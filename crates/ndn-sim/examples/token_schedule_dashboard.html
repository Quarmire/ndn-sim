The named token: does a demand-adaptive grant earn its keep?

Literal token-passing is a dead end for named-data radio — it needs an ordered ring of host identities, hard membership state, and a fragile passed frame on a lossy medium. But the token concept — a collision-free transmit grant — survives as a name-keyed, clock-derived slot (#61 in time, #40 in frequency). Fixed name-TDMA has token-ring's flaw, though: the grant rotates past idle names. The doctrine-compliant fix is a claimable slot — owned by name (t mod N) but, if the owner is idle, opened to a CCLF election among names that have data. No passed token, no host state. Here it's raced against pure fixed-TDMA and pure CCLF as the contention (# active names) grows.

A throughput vs contention
Demand-adaptive is the upper envelope

Delivered per slot as more names become active. TDMA rises linearly (wastes the idle slots); CCLF starts high but collapses as collisions climb; demand-adaptive tracks the better of the two everywhere — and at 8 active it beats both.

B worst-case access gap
Determinism, kept

p99 slots between a name's turns (log scale). TDMA and demand-adaptive hold a flat, bounded gap of N=16 — the owner's slot always comes. Pure CCLF's tail goes ragged, blowing out to 500+ slots as contention starves unlucky names.

C where the airtime goes · 8 of 16 names active
Idle waste, collision waste, or neither

Every slot is delivered content, a wasted idle slot, or a collision. TDMA burns half its slots idle; CCLF burns 59% on collisions; demand-adaptive reclaims the idle slots and pays only the collisions it can't avoid.
